# TestFlight and Versioning

## Internal distribution options, and where TestFlight sits among them

Apple's internal (non-App-Store) distribution methods form a taxonomy, and picking the wrong one for the team's size and goal is a common early mistake:

- **Personal Team** — the free-tier default; Development-only signing, a handful of devices, everything expiring in about a week. Learning/prototyping only, not a distribution strategy (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 5; see signing-and-provisioning.md for the exact free-tier caps on App IDs, devices, and profile lifetime).
- **Ad hoc** — paid-Program, up to 100 registered devices per product family per year, builds installed by manually shipping an `.ipa` or hosting it yourself. No Apple review gate at all. Wins on flexibility (bring your own OTA/third-party tooling) and speed (upload the moment you have an `.ipa`, no server-side processing wait) (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 5, ch. 6).
- **TestFlight** — paid-Program, Apple-hosted, deeply integrated with App Store Connect. Wins on simplicity: no linked framework, no UDID collection, and effectively zero ongoing maintenance burden compared to running your own OTA pipeline (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 6).
- **In-house / Custom App Distribution** — Apple Developer Enterprise Program territory, for large organizations distributing internal-use apps at a scale ad hoc's device cap can't reach. Apple now steers new deployments toward Custom App Distribution (built on the same App Store Connect/TestFlight infrastructure) and treats classic in-house distribution as the fallback for cases Custom App Distribution doesn't cover (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 5).

The judgment call for internal testing specifically — TestFlight vs. ad hoc — is a three-axis tradeoff: ad hoc wins on flexibility and speed, TestFlight wins on simplicity. Smaller teams tend to converge on TestFlight because the coordination overhead of managing UDIDs and rebuilding profiles isn't worth saving a few minutes of Apple's build-processing step; larger orgs with frequent builds or a need for custom OTA behavior (analytics hooks, staged rollouts of their own design) tend to graduate to ad hoc or a third-party layer over it (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 6). Security-conscious organizations that don't want any build — even a TestFlight build — leaving their own infrastructure sometimes choose self-hosted ad hoc distribution specifically to avoid putting pre-release binaries on Apple's (or any third party's) servers; that's a deliberate tradeoff of convenience for control, not a sign TestFlight is insecure (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 15).

None of this is available on a free/personal-team account — Personal Team categorically cannot upload a build to App Store Connect, so TestFlight (internal or external) requires enrollment in the paid Apple Developer Program regardless of team size (see signing-and-provisioning.md, "Free (personal team) vs paid Developer Program signing"). Document the full paid-tier TestFlight path below even when only a free account is available to verify mechanics locally.

## Internal vs. external testing

TestFlight testers split into two pools with materially different review, scale, and workflow implications (Distributing your app for beta testing and releases, https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/distributing-your-app-for-beta-testing-and-releases):

| | Internal testers | External testers |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Existing App Store Connect users on the team | Anyone invited by email or public link |
| Cap | Up to 100 people | Up to 10,000 people |
| Beta App Review | None — available as soon as the build finishes processing | Required for the first build added to a group; later builds may skip a full pass |
| Build expiration | 90 days from upload | 90 days from upload |

(App Store Connect Help, TestFlight overview, https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/test-a-beta-version/testflight-overview/; iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 5, ch. 6)

- **Internal testers** — must already be App Store Connect users on the team (Admin, App Manager, Developer, or similar role), capped at up to 100 people. No Beta App Review gate: internal builds become available to internal testers as soon as processing finishes, because internal testers are treated as part of the org, not the public (App Store Connect Help, TestFlight overview, https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/test-a-beta-version/testflight-overview/; iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 5, ch. 6).
- **External testers** — added either individually by email invite or by sharing a public sign-up link that anyone can use to join the beta, capped at up to 10,000 people per app (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 6). External builds *do* go through Beta App Review: the first build added to any external group is submitted to App Review to confirm it follows the App Review Guidelines before external testers can install it. Subsequent builds in that same testing cycle may not need a full review pass, but the gate exists and can reject or delay a build the same way a full submission can (App Store Connect Help, TestFlight overview; iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 6). Treat "add external testers" as a review-gated action, not a purely mechanical one — don't promise a same-hour external rollout the way you reasonably could for internal-only. A public link is the right choice when recruiting broadly (social media, an existing waitlist); individual email invites are the right choice when you need to know exactly who has access, e.g. an intake/application process for a beta with more interest than slots (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 6).
- **Build expiration** — every TestFlight build, internal or external, stops being testable 90 days after upload regardless of tester type. This is a hard ceiling, not a per-tester setting: plan beta cycles (and re-upload cadence) around it rather than discovering it mid-test (App Store Connect Help, TestFlight overview; iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 5, which independently corroborates the 90-day figure). If a build expires without being superseded, testers who already received an invitation can still pick it back up once you submit the same build to the App Store and it goes live — expiration blocks further TestFlight distribution, not a subsequent public release of that exact build (App Store Connect Help, TestFlight overview).
- **Platform nuance** — TestFlight for Mac requires the app to be built with Xcode 13 or later; there's no equivalent toolchain-version floor called out for the other platforms (App Store Connect Help, TestFlight overview).

Running a beta test well is coordination work, not technical work — recruiting testers (existing customers, a public sign-up link, or an application/intake process for high-interest betas), onboarding people who trip on the TestFlight app itself, and triaging incoming feedback and crash reports are where the real time goes; the TestFlight mechanics (invite, install, update) take minutes to set up and then get out of the way (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 6). Budget a person's ongoing attention for the test, not just an upload step. For the mechanics of reviewing tester feedback and crash/screenshot reports once they arrive, see Viewing and responding to feedback from beta testers (https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/viewing-and-responding-to-feedback) — the Feedback organizer in Xcode (and the app's TestFlight page in App Store Connect) is where screenshots, written feedback, and crash reports land, with per-item resolution tracking and team sharing built in.

## Testing the actual release build before distributing

A debug build run from Xcode is not a reliable proxy for what a tester or App Review will experience — treat a genuine Release-configuration archive as a distinct, required test target, not an artifact you only produce at upload time. Concretely, the two diverge in ways that hide real bugs behind a passing debug run: data migrations, keychain access, and defaults data behave differently on an app that's been previously installed versus a fresh one; the OS disables some features, throttles frame rate, or introduces delays under constrained device power, memory, or network conditions that a plugged-in simulator never exercises; and Xcode disables watchdog timeouts in debug builds, so a startup-time or main-thread hang that would get an app killed on a user's device can go completely unnoticed in daily development (Testing a release build, https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/testing-a-release-build).

To actually exercise this, set the scheme's run destination to a physical device and switch the archive action to the Release configuration before archiving — this is a distinct step from just distributing a debug build. For every distribution method except TestFlight (Ad Hoc, Enterprise, Development), also set App Thinning to "All compatible device variants" so the test build isn't silently restricted to your own device's variant, since App Thinning itself is a common source of device-specific bugs that a single-device test won't surface (Testing a release build). Xcode's archive-distribution flow exposes a dedicated **Release Testing** option (distinct from TestFlight and from Ad Hoc) specifically for this: it performs the same automatic code signing as an App Store distribution and exports the build to install on devices your team has registered, without submitting anything to TestFlight or the App Store — it is not available for Mac apps (Distributing your app for beta testing and releases). Before uploading anything, the Organizer's **Validate App** action runs Apple's automated checks against the archive without actually submitting it, catching configuration errors before they cost you a processing cycle (Distributing your app for beta testing and releases).

## `CFBundleVersion` vs `CFBundleShortVersionString`

These two `Info.plist` keys answer different questions and both are mandatory on every upload:

- **`CFBundleShortVersionString`** — the user-facing marketing version, shown on the product page and in Settings. Required format: three period-separated integers, `[Major].[Minor].[Patch]` (e.g. `2.4.1`), numeric characters and periods only (CFBundleShortVersionString, https://developer.apple.com/documentation/bundleresources/information-property-list/cfbundleshortversionstring).
- **`CFBundleVersion`** — the machine-readable build identifier, not shown to users. One to three period-separated integers in the same `[Major].[Minor].[Patch]` shape; extra components beyond three are accepted but ignored, and a short form is zero-padded (`10` means `10.0.0`, `10.5` means `10.5.0`). Required by the App Store and used system-wide to identify the specific build (CFBundleVersion, https://developer.apple.com/documentation/bundleresources/information-property-list/cfbundleversion).

```xml
<key>CFBundleShortVersionString</key>
<string>2.4.1</string>
<key>CFBundleVersion</key>
<string>37</string>
```

The discipline that matters operationally: **every single build uploaded to App Store Connect — whether it lands in TestFlight or goes straight to App Store submission — needs a `CFBundleVersion` that's unique and higher than any previous build App Store Connect has already accepted for that app.** App Store Connect rejects re-uploads that reuse a build number it has already seen; "bump the build number" before every archive is not busywork, it's the mechanism that lets App Store Connect tell two builds of the same marketing version apart (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 3). Xcode can automate this for you — "Manage version and build number" during the distribute/export flow updates the build number in the archive automatically (Distributing your app for beta testing and releases).

Two nuances worth calling out explicitly:

- The live `CFBundleVersion` reference singles out macOS with its own instruction to "increment the build version before you distribute a build" (CFBundleVersion) — and the reason for that platform split is confirmed elsewhere: App Store Connect requires each app version to use a *unique combination* of marketing version and build number, so iOS, iPadOS, tvOS, visionOS, and watchOS apps are free to reuse a lower build number on a new version than a previous one used, while Mac apps must keep their build number strictly increasing even across version bumps (Setting the next build number for Xcode Cloud builds, https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/setting-the-next-build-number-for-xcode-cloud-builds). Resetting a build-number sequence to `1` on every new marketing version is therefore a safe convention everywhere except macOS.
- Don't treat `CFBundleShortVersionString` as a free-form marketing string — the required numeric `A.B.C` format applies regardless of what you'd like to show users (a `"2.4 Beta"` string is not valid). If semantic versioning isn't already the team's convention, adopting it (major = breaking/new-feature release, minor = enhancement, patch = fix) gives the three components a stable meaning instead of ad hoc incrementing (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 3).

## Release-strategy judgment specific to TestFlight and versioning

Full phased-release mechanics (the 7-day percentage ramp) and the full expedited-review request process are documented in app-store-submission.md — don't restate the schedule or the request form here. What belongs in *this* file is the judgment that connects a TestFlight/versioning decision to those release levers:

- **A change validated only through internal TestFlight, with no external beta exposure, is a weaker candidate for skipping phased release than one that's had thousands of external testers on it for weeks.** Treat external TestFlight exposure as evidence that reduces the case for a cautious phased rollout — not a guarantee that lets you skip it, but a real input into the decision (see app-store-submission.md for how phased release itself works).
- **A build number bump alone is never a substitute for actually re-testing.** If a hotfix build's only difference from the last-tested build is the incremented `CFBundleVersion` and a one-line code change, that's the profile of a build that's a reasonable expedited-review candidate — small, well-understood, low blast radius. A build with substantial untested surface area is a poor fit for expedited review even if it's "urgent," because expedited review compresses App Review's time, not the risk of the change itself (see app-store-submission.md for the expedited-review request mechanics and Apple's own bar for granting one).
- **A fix discovered via TestFlight feedback, still on a beta build number, needs its own path to production** — either bump `CFBundleVersion` again and route the same content through one more TestFlight cycle before promoting it, or, if the bug is severe enough and already well-isolated, go straight to a new App Store submission (with its own new build number) and consider expedited review. Don't reuse a beta's build number for the App Store submission on the theory that "it was already tested" — App Store Connect's uniqueness rule (above) forces a new number regardless, and the version discipline exists precisely so the build that ships is traceably the one that was actually tested.

## When to run TestFlight externally, internal-only, or not at all

This is a judgment call, not a fixed policy — weigh it per release:

- **Run external TestFlight** when the change is large or risky enough that internal eyes won't surface the failure modes real users will hit (major UI rework, a new subscription flow, a migration touching stored user data), when broader pre-release validation across device/OS/locale diversity is worth the Beta App Review turnaround and the coordination overhead of recruiting and triaging feedback from up to 10,000 people, or when the beta itself is a marketing asset (early-access buzz, community goodwill) worth cultivating deliberately (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 6).
- **Stay internal-only** when the team is small, iteration needs to be fast, and the cost of Beta App Review's first-build gate and managing an external group outweighs the benefit — internal TestFlight still gives you the "does this actually work off my machine" signal without any external review latency (iOS App Distribution and Best Practises, ch. 5, ch. 6). This is also the right default while validating whether a change is even worth wider exposure before opening it up externally.
- **Skip TestFlight entirely** for a trivial, well-isolated hotfix where the fix is small enough to reason about directly (a copy fix, a one-line guard against a crash with an obvious root cause) and the cost of any beta cycle — even an internal one — is pure latency with no proportionate risk reduction. In that case, go straight to a new build and an App Store submission, using expedited review if the bug is actively harming users in production (see app-store-submission.md for that process). Reserve this path for changes small enough that "we tested it in TestFlight first" wouldn't have caught anything a code review didn't already catch.
